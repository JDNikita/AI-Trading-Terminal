**Buy the Rumor, Sell the News: Event Mechanics and Product Launch Price Action**
=====================================================================================

As a quantitative portfolio manager, understanding the mechanics behind "Buy the Rumor, Sell the News" (BTRS) events is crucial for navigating product launches and managing risk. This section will delve into the three phases of BTRS events, providing a comprehensive understanding of the price action and mechanics involved.

### Phase 1: Pre-announcement accumulation (T-21 to T-7 days) and Volatility Compression

**T-21 to T-14 days:**
-------------------------

*   **Accumulation phase:** Institutional investors, such as hedge funds and family offices, begin to accumulate shares of the upcoming product launch.
*   **Volatility compression:** As institutional investors buy, they compress the volatility of the stock, creating a "buy the rumor" environment.
*   **Price action:** The stock price increases, often with a high volume of trades, as institutional investors drive the price higher.

**T-14 to T-10 days:**
-------------------------

*   **Increased buying pressure:** Institutional investors continue to accumulate shares, increasing buying pressure on the stock.
*   **Volatility reduction:** The compressed volatility creates a sense of stability, making it more difficult for bears to short the stock.
*   **Price action:** The stock price continues to rise, often with a decrease in volatility.

**T-10 to T-7 days:**
-------------------------

*   **Final accumulation phase:** Institutional investors make their final purchases, driving the price to new highs.
*   **Volatility compression:** The stock price is now at an all-time high, and institutional investors are reluctant to sell.
*   **Price action:** The stock price reaches its peak, often with a high volume of trades.

### Phase 2: Announcement Day Exhaustion (T-0) and Distribution into Retail Buying

**T-0:**
---------

*   **Exhaustion phase:** Institutional investors sell their shares, creating a "sell the news" environment.
*   **Distribution into retail buying:** Retail investors, who had been waiting for the product launch, begin to buy the stock.
*   **Intraday reversals:** The stock price reverses, often with a long upper wick, as retail investors drive the price higher.
*   **Price action:** The stock price rises, often with a high volume of trades, as retail investors buy.

### Phase 3: Post-release Retracement Math (50% to 61.8% retracements)

**50% Retracement:**
-------------------

*   **Initial retracement:** The stock price falls by 50% from its peak, often with a long lower wick.
*   **Bullish reversal:** The stock price begins to rise again, often with a short upper wick, as the retracement is seen as a buying opportunity.

**61.8% Retracement:**
----------------------

*   **Extended retracement:** The stock price falls by 61.8% from its peak, often with a long lower wick.
*   **Bearish reversal:** The stock price begins to rise again, often with a short upper wick, as the retracement is seen as a buying opportunity.

By recognizing the three phases of BTRS events, traders and investors can make informed decisions about buying and selling shares. Whether you're a seasoned trader or a new investor, this comprehensive guide provides a solid foundation for navigating the complex world of BTRS events.

3. The Overbought Anomaly and order book collapse mechanics during liquidity cliffs

### 1. Liquidity Voids during Macro News Releases and the 3-Day Institutional Follow-Through Rule

Macro news releases, such as the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) decisions, can create significant liquidity voids in the market. These voids can lead to a surge in institutional trading activity, as market participants attempt to capitalize on the news.

The 3-day institutional follow-through rule is a widely observed phenomenon, where institutional investors tend to wait 3 days after a macro news release before entering or exiting positions. This rule is based on the idea that institutional investors need time to process the news and adjust their positions accordingly.

However, this rule can be flawed in parabolic regimes, where the market is already heavily leveraged and sensitive to news. In such cases, the 3-day wait period can be too long, allowing the market to continue its momentum and potentially leading to further losses.

### 2. Memecoin Exponential Attention Feedback Loops and Social Media Volume Velocity

Memecoin sentiment velocity is a critical factor in determining the price movement of these cryptocurrencies. Memecoin prices are often driven by social media volume velocity, which refers to the rate at which social media platforms such as Twitter and Reddit are used to discuss and promote memecoin-related content.

Memecoin exponential attention feedback loops occur when a small increase in social media volume velocity leads to a larger increase in price, which in turn leads to an even larger increase in social media volume velocity. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle, where the price movement becomes increasingly dependent on social media sentiment.

The exponential attention feedback loop can be broken when social media volume velocity reaches a peak, leading to a decrease in price and a subsequent decrease in social media volume velocity. However, in parabolic regimes, this feedback loop can be difficult to break, leading to a continued increase in price and a further increase in social media volume velocity.

### 3. The Overbought Anomaly and Order Book Collapse Mechanics during Liquidity Cliffs

The Overbought Anomaly is a phenomenon where the Relative Strength Index (RSI) exceeds 80, indicating that the market is overbought and due for a correction. However, in parabolic regimes, the Overbought Anomaly can fail to trigger a mean-reversion, as the market becomes increasingly sensitive to news and events.

When the RSI exceeds 80, it can lead to an order book collapse, where the market becomes flooded with buy orders and sell orders, leading to a liquidity cliff. This can result in a sharp increase in price, as the market becomes increasingly desperate to buy or sell.

The order book collapse mechanics during liquidity cliffs can be broken when the market reaches a critical point, where the number of buy and sell orders becomes too large to be accommodated by the market makers. This can lead to a sharp decrease in price, as the market becomes flooded with sell orders and the market makers are unable to absorb the sell pressure.
